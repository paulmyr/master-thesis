\section{The benchmark headline as a negative result}\label{sec:discussion:headline}

The recovery-radius benchmark of \cref{sec:results:baseline} produces an unambiguous, and uncomfortable, picture.
The three gradient-based configurations cluster within a narrow band of $P(\Gamma>0.5)$ that overlaps the no-optimisation floor on every scenario; only \texttt{grad\_vanrossum} on \texttt{adaptation} achieves a recovery radius strictly larger than the floor ($\delta_{50}=0.05$ vs.\ floor $\delta_{50}=0.02$).
\texttt{grad\_mse} sits at or below the floor everywhere.
\texttt{nm\_guarino}, by contrast, has a recovery radius two to four grid tiers wider than any gradient method on every scenario ($\delta_{50}\in\{0.2, 0.2, 0.4\}$).
The plain reading of these numbers is that, with the loss functions, surrogate, and stabilisation techniques studied here, the gradient signal does not enlarge the basin of attraction relative to a derivative-free simplex on the same objective.
The rest of this chapter discusses why.

\section{Surrogate gradients as biased estimators}\label{sec:discussion:bias}

A surrogate gradient is not an approximation of the true gradient: the true gradient of a loss involving a Heaviside spike indicator is zero almost everywhere, so there is nothing to approximate.
What the optimiser actually descends on is the exact gradient of a \emph{smoothed} model, in which the threshold step is replaced by a sigmoid of width $\sim 1/\beta$~\cite{Neftci2019}.
This smoothed loss $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta)$ and the true loss $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ agree in the limit $\beta\to\infty$, so their minima coincide \emph{asymptotically};
at any finite $\beta$, however, the surrogate gradient is a biased estimator of the true minimum.
It points toward minima of $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}$, not $\mathcal{L}$, and there is no guarantee that the two sets are close at the precision required to fit individual spike times.

This produces the well-known dead-neuron trade-off characterised by Gygax \& Zenke~\cite{Gygax2025}.
A sharp surrogate ($\beta\gtrsim 25$) makes $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}$ a tight envelope of $\mathcal{L}$, but the active window of the gradient shrinks to the order of $1/\beta$ in voltage units --- on the AdEx voltage range a $\sim$0.4\,mV window, consistent with the surrogate kernels of \cref{fig:surr_grads} and the gradient-magnitude spread reported in \cref{tab:surrogate-gradient}.
A broad surrogate ($\beta\sim 5$, the setting we use throughout) restores informative gradients but the minima of $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}$ are displaced from those of $\mathcal{L}$ by a quantity that the optimiser cannot resolve.
The benchmark numbers in \cref{sec:results:baseline} suggest that this displacement is large relative to the basin of attraction we would need to claim reliable parameter recovery: gradient methods succeed only where the perturbed initial point is already close enough to the true optimum that the bias does not move the recovered $\hat\theta$ outside the $\Gamma>0.5$ region.

Two additional structural problems compound the bias.
First, even when the per-step surrogate is well calibrated, $\sim$5000 timesteps of leak dynamics multiply many small backward terms; the resulting gradient at the parameters is dominated by numerical noise.
Second, most published evidence for surrogate gradients comes from population-level \gls{SNN} classification tasks, where the bias is averaged over many neurons and across loss invariances induced by the readout layer~\cite{Neftci2019, Zenke2018, Perez-Nieves2021}.
In single-neuron \gls{AdEx} parameter fitting against an exact trace neither averaging mechanism is available, so the bias propagates directly into $\hat\theta$.
This framing is consistent with what the benchmark shows: the optimiser is genuinely converging --- the loss does decrease, gradients are non-zero --- but it is converging on a slightly wrong objective, and the displacement between the surrogate's optimum and the true optimum is roughly the size of the gradient-method recovery radius.

This bias view also predicts why \texttt{grad\_vanrossum} performs best of the three gradient losses on \texttt{adaptation}.
Van Rossum convolves a binary spike train with a smooth exponential kernel before computing $L^{2}$ distance;
the convolution makes the loss surface itself smooth in $s(t)$, so the only source of surrogate bias is the path from $\theta$ to $s(t)$, not the path from $s(t)$ to $\mathcal{L}$.
Feature-based losses with sigmoid-gated validity flags compound a second softening layer on top of the surrogate, which can plausibly add to the displacement.
We cannot quantify either contribution directly from this benchmark, but the empirical ordering --- Van Rossum $\geq$ Guarino $\geq$ MSE under gradient descent, with all three behind Nelder--Mead --- is consistent with the bias hypothesis.

\section{Why Nelder--Mead pulls ahead}\label{sec:discussion:nm}

The recovery-radius benchmark introduces two Nelder--Mead variants on purpose: \texttt{nm\_guarino}, which evaluates the same soft-feature loss that \texttt{grad\_guarino} sees, and \texttt{nm\_guarino\_hard}, which evaluates the exact-feature Guarino loss that derivative-free pipelines (including Guarino et al.~\cite{Guarino2025}) normally use.
Comparing both to the gradient methods isolates two effects that the soft-only \texttt{nm\_guarino} alone confounds: the change of optimiser (gradient $\to$ simplex) and the change of objective (soft $\to$ hard features).

Two consequences follow from replacing gradient descent with the simplex while holding the objective fixed.
First, Nelder--Mead does not see the surrogate-induced smoothed loss in the same way: it interacts with $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}$ (because that is what is evaluated under the surrogate-enabled forward pass), but it does so as a black-box optimiser, with no claim that the local slope it estimates from polled corners of its simplex matches an analytic derivative.
On a piecewise constant landscape with narrow informative valleys, this is an advantage: the simplex can step over plateaus that gradient descent gets stuck on (\cref{sec:results:landscape}).
Second, derivative-free methods are insensitive to the gradient-magnitude pathologies that motivated our stabilisation toolbox in the first place (\cref{sec:training-stabilization}).
Polyak normalisation, sigmoid reparameterisation, and surrogate calibration are responses to vanishing/exploding gradients, exploding parameter scales, and the dead-neuron problem;
Nelder--Mead simply does not encounter them.

The change of objective sharpens the diagnosis.
The hard-feature variant \texttt{nm\_guarino\_hard} matches the soft variant cell-for-cell on every scenario in the recovery-radius table (\cref{sec:results:nm-soft-vs-hard}):
identical $\delta_{50}\in\{0.2,0.2,0.4\}$, identical median $\Gamma_{0.05}=+1.00$, and per-direction outcomes that differ in at most one of thirty directions per cell.
Empirically the soft features are a faithful surrogate for the exact Guarino objective at the precision the benchmark probes.
This is the directly informative finding for the comparison against the gradient methods, because it rules out the natural objection that \texttt{nm\_guarino} is competing on a different (smoothed) objective.
The objective is effectively the same;
the difference is the gradient consumer.

The implication for the thesis question --- \emph{can gradient-based parameter optimisation produce useful parameter updates for the \gls{AdEx} model?} --- is that the surrogate gradient, not the soft features, is what separates the gradient methods from the simplex.
Soft features were introduced to make the loss differentiable, and they do that without distorting the objective in a way the simplex can detect at this budget;
what fails is the descent that the surrogate gradient supplies on top of that objective.
The bias argument of \cref{sec:discussion:bias} is therefore not just one possible explanation among several --- it is the one explanation remaining once the soft-feature confound is removed by the hard-feature control.
Per-parameter recovery (\cref{fig:recovery-per-param}) confirms the same conclusion from a different angle: when either NM variant succeeds, it succeeds on the same parameters that the gradient methods get right at small $\delta$.
The two families of methods do not have qualitatively different parameter-recovery profiles, only quantitatively different recovery radii.
This is what we would expect if both families are limited by the loss landscape and only differ in how robustly they navigate it.

\section{Klos \& Memmesheimer's exact spike-time gradients}\label{sec:discussion:klos}

A complementary line of work~\cite{Klos2025} sidesteps the surrogate-gradient bias entirely by changing the neuron model.
Their construction switches from \gls{LIF} to a \gls{QIF}-family model in which $\dot V$ becomes infinite at threshold, making spike times continuously differentiable functions of the parameters with closed-form between-spike solutions.
A separate \emph{pseudospike} mechanism extends the dynamics past trial end so that silent neurons still have an addressable (pseudo)spike time, removing the dead-neuron problem by construction rather than by surrogate calibration.

This approach is not directly transferable to the \gls{AdEx} setting we study.
\gls{AdEx} has a steep but \emph{finite} slope at threshold (the exponential term clamped at $\exp(10)$ in our implementation, \cref{sec:method:adex_impl}), so its spike-time-as-function-of-parameters relation is differentiable in a measure-zero sense only;
implementing the Klos--Memmesheimer construction would require closed-form solutions of the \gls{AdEx} dynamics between spikes, which do not exist due to the coupling to the adaptation current $w$.
\gls{AdEx} therefore occupies an intermediate position --- between the \gls{LIF} regime, where surrogate gradients are essential, and the \gls{QIF} regime, where they are unnecessary --- and the relatively benign behaviour we observe at small $\delta$ for gradient methods may reflect this intermediate slope rather than any property of the surrogate.
We note this as a hypothesis: the benchmark of \cref{sec:results:baseline} would gain quantitative meaning if it were rerun with a \gls{LIF} channel substituted for \gls{AdEx}, but that comparison is outside the scope of this thesis.

The implication for future work is the cleanest takeaway from this chapter.
If exact recovery of spike times matters more than fidelity to a specific biophysical model, switching to a \gls{QIF}-family neuron with the Klos--Memmesheimer construction is a strictly better starting point than refining surrogates on \gls{AdEx}.
If the converse is true and the biophysical specifics of \gls{AdEx} are required, the present results suggest that loss-landscape design --- multi-trace constraints, curriculum schedules, hybrid losses that combine the smoothness of Van Rossum with the temporal credit assignment of feature-based approaches --- is a more productive direction than further work on the optimiser side.
