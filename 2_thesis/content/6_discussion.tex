\section{Simplified Neurons in Jaxley}\label{sec:discussion:jaxley}

Independently of the optimization results that occupy the rest of this chapter, the implementation contribution stands on its own.
We extended \texttt{Jaxley}, still in early development, with a new \gls{AdEx} channel\footnote{We were able to contribute it to Jaxley, see \href{https://github.com/jaxleyverse/jaxley/pull/780}{PR}} (\cref{sec:method:adex_impl,sec:results:verification}).
More substantively, were the first\mwarning{Missing subject: ``were the first'' $\to$ ``we were the first''.} to try a surrogate-gradient mechanism for any of its simplified models for parameter fitting.
The \texttt{custom\_vjp} construction generalizes beyond \gls{AdEx}: the same pattern --- voltage integration inside \texttt{update\_states}, surrogate \gls{VJP} on the spike indicator --- applies verbatim to the pre-existing \gls{LIF} and Izhikevich channels.
There is still lots of movement and structural changes\mwarning{Agreement: ``There is\dots{}changes'' $\to$ ``There are still lots of movement and structural changes''.} to be expected within the framework.
The development team plans to publish a different surrogate gradient implementation with the \texttt{Jaxley} v1.0.0 release.\iwarning{Forward-looking claim with no citation --- add a source (issue/PR/roadmap) or soften.}

The practical leverage of this is broader than the gradient-fitting story \texttt{Jaxley} was originally built for.
Differentiability of biophysical models is one of its headline features, but the same simulator --- \gls{JIT}-compiled, \texttt{vmap}-batched, GPU-capable --- is equally useful for derivative-free pipelines that consume many cheap forward passes: evolutionary algorithms, parameter-space exploration as in Guarino et al.~\cite{Guarino2025}, and simulation-based inference~\cite{Goncalves2020}.
The infrastructure is therefore valuable independently of whether the gradient consumer ultimately delivers on the recovery problem --- an observation that becomes material in \cref{sec:discussion:diffadex}, where a derivative-free baseline running on this same simulator outperforms the gradient methods it was originally built to support.

\section{Differentiable AdEx for parameter optimization doesn't work}\label{sec:discussion:diffadex}

The recovery-radius benchmark of \cref{sec:results:baseline} produces an unambiguous, and uncomfortable, picture.
Across all three scenarios and over the full range of perturbation scales, no gradient method matched the success rate of the Nelder--Mead simplex on the same objective; in most cells the gradient method scored at or below the no-optimization initialization floor.
\gls{MSE} was actively harmful (every run finished at or below the floor); Guarino tied the floor's recovery radius without exceeding it on any scenario\iwarning{On \texttt{adaptation}, \texttt{grad\_guarino}'s $\delta_{50}$ is ``--'' (below the floor's $0.02$), so ``tied\dots{}on any scenario'' is inaccurate there.}; Van Rossum, the most promising of the three, enlarged the basin only on \texttt{adaptation}.
The implication for the thesis question is direct: gradient-based parameter optimization produces useful parameter updates for the \gls{AdEx} model only inside basins of attraction that are already small enough for derivative-free search to cover.
The argument that initially motivated this line of work --- that the order-of-magnitude speedups Deistler et al.~\cite{Deistler2025} report for \gls{HH}-type fitting should transfer to simplified models once surrogate gradients are added --- does not survive contact with the benchmark.
Gradient fitting works for \gls{HH}-type models not because the optimization problem is structurally similar to \gls{AdEx} with a softening patch applied at one node, but because \gls{HH} dynamics are smooth from end to end and \gls{AdEx} dynamics are not.

The natural objection --- that the gradient methods lose because the soft Guarino features distort the objective in a way the simplex does not see --- is ruled out empirically.
The two Nelder--Mead variants, \texttt{nm\_guarino} (soft features, identical loss surface to \texttt{grad\_guarino}) and \texttt{nm\_guarino\_hard} (exact threshold-crossing features), report identical recovery radii on every scenario, with per-direction outcomes differing by at most one direction in thirty.
What separates the two families of methods is therefore not the objective but the gradient consumer.
The simplex treats the loss surface as a black box: it estimates local slope by polling the corners of its simplex rather than from an analytic derivative, so on a piecewise-constant landscape with narrow informative valleys it can step over the plateaus that defeat surrogate-mediated descent, and it never encounters the gradient-magnitude pathologies that motivated our stabilization toolbox (\cref{sec:method:opt:stabilization}) in the first place.
Gradient descent does encounter all of them.
The stabilization toolbox addresses gradient \emph{magnitude} pathologies; the dominant pathologies on the gradient side are about direction and existence, which magnitude fixes cannot reach.
We identify three candidate failure modes, each of which would individually be sufficient to produce the benchmark gap.

\subsection{Failure mode 1: Surrogate gradients as biased estimators}\label{sec:discussion:bias}

The ``approximate gradient'' framing of surrogate gradients is misleading.
$\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \theta$ involves the Heaviside derivative and is zero almost everywhere, so there is no true gradient to approximate.
What the optimizer descends on is the exact gradient of a different objective --- a smoothed loss $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_\beta(\theta)$ obtained by integrating the surrogate kernel back through the threshold step, so the hard threshold is effectively replaced by a smooth step of width $\sim 1/\beta$~\cite{Gygax2025}.
The minima of $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_\beta$ and $\mathcal{L}$ coincide only as $\beta\to\infty$;
at any finite $\beta$ they are separated by a displacement that scales with $1/\beta$.
This displacement is the bias: gradient descent converges cleanly to minima of $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_\beta$, which need not align with those of $\mathcal{L}$ at the precision required to fit individual spike times.

The bias is invisible in the setting where surrogate gradients were originally validated.
\gls{SNN} classification averages it across many neurons and across the symmetries of a readout layer~\cite{Neftci2019,Zenke2018,Perez-Nieves2021}: the recovered weight set is itself underdetermined, so a small displacement in any one $\hat w_i$ does not change the task loss.
In single-neuron \gls{AdEx} fitting against an exact target trace neither averaging mechanism is available, so the displacement lands directly on $\hat\theta$ and surfaces as a finite recovery radius even though the gradient is non-zero and the loss decreases.

The Van Rossum $\geq$ Guarino ranking in \cref{tab:results-summary}\ierror{Not supported by the table: on \texttt{tonic} ($\Gamma_{0.05}=+0.07$ vs $+0.13$) and \texttt{initial\_bursting} ($+0.12$ vs $+0.19$) Guarino $>$ Van Rossum; VR only wins on \texttt{adaptation}. The clean ordering claim overreaches --- soften to ``VR is the least-biased of the three'' or similar.} is consistent with this view, in that it orders the losses by how many softening layers stack on top of the surrogate.
Van Rossum sees the surrogate as its only source of bias --- the kernel convolution and the $L^2$ norm are analytically differentiable --- whereas Guarino additionally routes through soft validity gates whose sharpness $\beta_v$ adds a second softening layer with its own calibration knob.
\gls{MSE}'s position at the bottom of the same table is not explained by this view; it is taken up in \cref{sec:discussion:landscape-fm}.

\subsection{Failure mode 2: Loss-landscape pathologies differ per loss}\label{sec:discussion:landscape-fm}

The per-loss artefacts catalogued in \cref{sec:results:landscape} have a unifying property: they are properties of the loss formulation, not of the surrogate.
None of them would be repaired by replacing the surrogate with an exact spike-time gradient.

For \gls{MSE}, eliminating a misaligned spike reduces pointwise voltage error more than aligning it, so the non-spiking trace is a genuine minimum of the loss itself (\cref{sec:results:landscape:mse}); an exact gradient would point in the same wrong direction.
For Guarino, the missing-feature penalty introduces a step in the \emph{forward} loss at the boundary between the no-spike and few-spikes regimes, which the validity gates smooth only locally; the ridge that defeats descent in \cref{sec:results:landscape:guarino} is in the loss surface, not in its gradient computation.
For Van Rossum, spike-count changes are properties of $s(t)$ that the kernel convolution operates downstream of, so they remain a source of discrete jumps in $K\ast s$ regardless of how the gradient through $s(t)$ is computed (\cref{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum}).

Guarino additionally carries a calibration cost the others do not.
Four softness hyperparameters --- feature weights, missing-feature penalty magnitudes, validity-gate sharpness $\beta_v$, and surrogate $\beta$ --- jointly determine the loss landscape and the gradient direction, and they have to be set per cell.
A derivative-free pipeline pays none of this cost, because exact thresholds and integer spike counts are valid feature definitions when no gradient is required.

The implication for the chapter is symmetrical to FM1's.
Where FM1 said the consumer is biased towards minima of the wrong objective, FM2 says the objective itself is hostile to gradient descent in ways no surrogate change can fix.

\subsection{Failure mode 3: Vanishing gradients through long unrolled simulation}\label{sec:discussion:decay}

Even with a clean per-step surrogate (FM1 removed) and a friendly loss surface (FM2 removed), the parameter gradient still has to survive $\sim 5000$ multiplicative leak-Jacobian terms, which \cref{sec:bg:sg:vanishing-gradients} establishes as a severe vanishing regime for the \gls{AdEx} parameters that affect subthreshold dynamics.

The standard mitigations from the \gls{SNN} literature presume something the present setting does not have.
Truncated \gls{BPTT} trades signal length for an additional approximation bias on top of the FM1 bias the chapter is already trying to argue against.
Eligibility-trace methods~\cite{Bellec2020} avoid backward-in-time propagation but are formulated for recurrent networks with a per-step error signal, which single-trace voltage-recording fitting does not provide.
Increasing the integration step $\Delta t$ shrinks the unroll depth at the cost of accuracy on the exponential spike-initiation term --- the regime in which the surrogate kernel does most of its work --- so the trade is not benign.

This failure mode is the most speculative of the three.
Its effect is confounded with FM1 and FM2 at every gradient step, so the benchmark cannot isolate it; the theoretical case is strong but the empirical signature --- a residual radius gap that persists after FM1 and FM2 are removed --- is outside the scope of this thesis to test.

The three failure modes interact at every gradient step: the surrogate bias creates the displacement, the landscape pathologies determine whether the (biased) minimum can be navigated to, and the gradient decay determines whether the signal survives the trip backward.
The present data cannot apportion their relative weight, but the negative conclusion is robust to whichever turns out to dominate.
The cleanest forward direction this leaves is not a better surrogate or a better loss but a different neuron model --- one that avoids the bias by construction rather than trying to calibrate around it.
We return to that argument in \cref{sec:discussion:klos} with the Klos \& Memmesheimer~\cite{Klos2025} construction.

The cleanest alternative to the surrogate-bias problem above is to avoid the bias rather than calibrate around it: change the neuron model so the bias does not exist.
The Klos--Memmesheimer construction~\cite{Klos2025} introduced in \cref{sec:rw:sg-snn} does exactly this, with closed-form between-spike solutions for the \gls{QIF}-family and a pseudospike mechanism that keeps silent neurons addressable.
Where our gradient methods descend on a smoothed loss $\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}$ and hope it tracks $\mathcal{L}$ closely enough, theirs descend on $\mathcal{L}$ itself.

\gls{AdEx} sits awkwardly between the two regimes.
The exponential term (clamped at $\exp(10)$ in our implementation, \cref{sec:method:adex_impl}) makes the slope at threshold steep but finite, and the coupling to the adaptation current $w$ rules out the closed-form between-spike solutions the Klos--Memmesheimer construction requires.
The intermediate slope may also explain why our gradient methods remain at least partially functional at small $\delta$ in \cref{sec:results:baseline}: surrogate bias scales with how sharply the forward pass departs from the smoothed model, and \gls{AdEx} is much closer to \gls{QIF} than to \gls{LIF} on that axis.
A direct diagnostic --- rerunning the benchmark with a \gls{LIF} channel substituted for \gls{AdEx}, where the slope is genuinely zero and the bias should dominate --- is outside the scope of this thesis but is the experiment that would confirm or falsify the bias view most cleanly.

The choice this leaves for downstream work depends on what the simplified-neuron pipeline is for.
If exact recovery of spike times matters more than fidelity to a specific biophysical model, the \gls{QIF}-family with the Klos--Memmesheimer construction is a strictly better starting point than refining surrogates on \gls{AdEx}.
If the biophysical specifics of \gls{AdEx} are required, the bottleneck is the loss landscape, not the optimizer:
multi-trace constraints (\cref{sec:method:opt:stabilization:multi-trace}), curriculum schedules, 
hybrid objectives that combine Van Rossum's kernel smoothness with feature-based temporal credit assignment,
and signal-processing-derived spike-train distances we have not explored are all more productive avenues
than further work on the gradient-consumer side.
\Cref{ch:conclusionsAndFutureWork} returns to these directions as concrete recommendations.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:discussion:limitations}
The negative result above are\mwarning{Agreement + repetition: ``The negative result above are the results'' $\to$ ``The negative result above is the outcome''.} the results of a benchmark whose scope is deliberately narrow.
Five constraints in particular bound the conclusion's magnitude.

\textbf{Single model.}
The recovery-radius benchmark is on \gls{AdEx} only.
The bias argument of \cref{sec:discussion:bias} predicts that gradient methods should fare worse on \gls{LIF} (slope zero at threshold, surrogate bias maximal) and somewhere between \gls{AdEx} and \gls{QIF} on Izhikevich; neither prediction is tested.
Substituting \texttt{FireSurrogate} (or an Izhikevich equivalent) into the benchmark pipeline is a drop-in change and is the cleanest way to falsify the surrogate-bias view.

\textbf{Single stimulus protocol.}
All scenarios use $100$\,ms step currents.
Time-varying or ramped inputs might exercise gradient pathways through $\tau_w$ that step currents cannot, and the multi-trace setup of \cref{sec:method:opt:stabilization:multi-trace} is implemented but not used in the headline benchmark.
A multi-trace replication of the recovery-radius experiment is the natural extension and would speak directly to the adaptation-parameter identifiability question deferred in \cref{sec:method:opt:recovery-benchmark:scope}.
However, parameter recovery on a single trace for small perturbations will be almost certainly\mwarning{Word order: ``will be almost certainly'' $\to$ ``will almost certainly be''.} a necessary precondition for any successful multi-trace optimizations.

\textbf{Fixed hyperparameters per method.}
Each method runs with one configuration across all scenarios and perturbations.
No per-cell tuning, no architecture search.
Guarino's hyperparameter brittleness (\cref{sec:discussion:landscape-fm}) is itself evidence that an \gls{HP}-search budget could shift the gradient-method radii up;
we have not quantified by how much, and this is the most likely angle from which a follow-up could partially overturn the result.
However, if scenario specific hyperparameter tuning is needed, to successfully run this method, one could directly sample over the initial parameter space using the same computational resources.

\textbf{Failure modes as candidates, not isolated causes.}
The three failure modes are entangled at every gradient step.
Isolating FM1 cleanly requires the LIF substitution above;
isolating FM3 requires sweeping simulation length while holding FM1 and FM2 fixed;
isolating FM2 requires an exact-gradient method on \gls{AdEx}, which is the construction that does not exist for this model family.
The chapter's negative conclusion is robust to whichever component dominates, but the diagnosis itself is not substantiated by isolation experiments.

\textbf{Synthetic targets.}
The benchmark uses model-generated data, so it measures the optimization problem in isolation rather than the model--data mismatch one would face on real recordings.
The recovery radii reported are upper bounds on what the same setup would achieve experimentally.
Since no perfect recovery can be expected when fitting to real neurons, a (further) drastic drop in fit-ability\mwarning{``fit-ability'' is informal --- reword, e.g. ``fit quality'' or ``recoverability''.} can be expected.
