\section{An AdEx Channel for Jaxley}\label{sec:results:verification}

The first concrete outcome of this thesis is a working \gls{AdEx} channel for \texttt{Jaxley}, validated against an established reference simulator.
Following the protocol of \cref{sec:verification}, we compare our implementation to Brian2 \cite{Stimberg2019} on all three reference parameter sets from Naud et al.~\cite{Naud2008} --- tonic, adaptation and original --- which together cover the qualitatively distinct firing regimes \gls{AdEx} is meant to reproduce.

Across all three configurations, the two simulators produce identical spike counts, and the maximum pair-wise spike-timing difference stays below $1$\,ms.
\Cref{fig:brian_comparison} shows the side-by-side comparison of voltage traces, adaptation current $w$, and spike times.
The remaining sub-millisecond differences are what one would expect given that the two simulators use different integration schemes for the membrane equation, and they are well below the temporal resolution at which we later evaluate spike-timing accuracy via the coincidence factor $\Gamma$.

\begin{figure*}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/adex_comparison_combined.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Comparison of our \texttt{Jaxley} \gls{AdEx} implementation against Brian2 across three reference parameter sets.
Left to right: tonic, adaptation and original parameters.
Tonic and adaptation parameters refer to the parameters provided by \cite{Naud2008}, original parameters are the parameters used in the original AdEx paper \cite{Brette2005}.
Top row shows the simulated voltage traces;
note that when the threshold is reached, the voltage is reset within the same time step --- a behaviour that becomes important when thinking about loss functions later on.
Second row displays the adaptation current $w$.
Last row compares the spike timings between \texttt{Brian2} and \texttt{Jaxley}.}\label{fig:brian_comparison}

This validation matters for two reasons.
First, it establishes that an \gls{AdEx}-style simplified neuron --- with its discrete reset and exponential spike initiation --- can be expressed cleanly inside a framework otherwise designed for \gls{HH}-type compartmental models.
The same implementation pattern carries over to other simplified models such as \gls{LIF} and Izhikevich, so the contribution is not strictly limited to \gls{AdEx}.
Second, and more importantly for the rest of this chapter: every optimisation failure we report from here on can be discussed as a property of gradient-based training and loss-function design, rather than as a possible artefact of the underlying dynamics.
The forward model is not the bottleneck.

\section{Loss Function Analysis}\label{sec:results:landscape}

We implemented various different loss functions:
\gls{MSE}, a summary statistics-based loss (like Deistler used), \gls{SDTW}, Van Rossum distance and a typically feature-based loss --- this implementation is based on Guarino et al. \cite{Guarino2025}.
We were only able to fit the AdEx model using the latter two in isolated instances.
With \gls{MSE}, \gls{SDTW}, summary statistics and other loss functions, we were not successful in any test whatsoever.

To better understand these loss functions, we plotted loss landscapes.
We fixed the parameters at the adaptive scenario described in \ref{}\mtodo{Table with adaptive NAUD spiking paramters}.
Then, we varied two parameters in an 20x20 grid, bounded by the parameter bounds.
\Cref{sec:app:loss} (appendix) shows all 28 surfaces for the feature-based loss.
The qualitative picture repeats across loss functions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Massive non-spiking plateaus.} Large regions of parameter space produce no spikes at all and yield a constant (or near-constant) loss. For the feature-based loss this plateau value exceeds $10^{5}$ and dominates the total volume of the grid.
  \item \textbf{Narrow low-loss valleys.} The spiking region forms thin, curved valleys rather than isotropic basins. Valleys are often parallel to one axis (e.g.\ $g_L$--$E_L$ trades off along a single direction), meaning small updates along the wrong axis leave the loss unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Gradient direction outside the valley.} Because the plateau is flat, gradients on the plateau are dominated by soft spike-detection terms rather than by the true distance to a spiking solution. The optimiser therefore cannot recover once it has been pushed onto the plateau.
\end{itemize}

\section{Parameter Recovery by Loss Function}\label{sec:results:training}

We evaluated each loss function on a synthetic single-trace recovery task.
Ground-truth parameters are fixed at one of the Naud reference sets (tonic, adaptation, or original)~\cite{Naud2008},
a target voltage trace is generated with the \texttt{Jaxley} \gls{AdEx} implementation validated in \cref{sec:results:verification},
and the trainable parameters are perturbed before optimisation.
Because the optimiser has access to the same forward model that produced the target,
any failure to recover the parameters is attributable to the loss function and the optimisation procedure rather than to model mismatch.

The headline result is mixed.
We were not able to recover adaption parameter $a, b, \tau_w$.
Gradient-based fitting can recover the other \gls{AdEx} Parameters in all of these regimes when peturbations are small --- we open the section with one such success in \cref{sec:results:positive-example} as a worked example to establish that the approach is viable in principle.
However, recovery is fragile:
each loss required its own hand-tuned combination of learning rate, surrogate slope, and gradient clipping,
and even with careful tuning we could not identify a single hyperparameter set that recovers parameters consistently across regimes and seeds.
The remainder of the section unpacks this asymmetry one loss at a time.
\Cref{sec:results:mse} shows that \gls{MSE} collapses to a non-spiking solution on every regime we tested;
\cref{sec:results:guarino} shows that the feature-based Guarino loss can match spike counts but not timing;
and \cref{sec:results:vanrossum} shows that the Van Rossum distance recovers timing when it converges,
but inherits the non-spiking-plateau failure mode diagnosed in \cref{sec:results:landscape}.
\Cref{sec:results:baseline} then aggregates over many seeds and quantifies the overall failure rate, alongside a derivative-free baseline.

\subsection{Mean Squared Error}\label{sec:results:mse}

The \gls{MSE} loss on raw voltage traces fails as a parameter-fitting objective.
The optimiser consistently converges to a non-spiking subthreshold solution that tracks the resting potential of the target trace (\cref{fig:mse-training-results}a) and drives the loss monotonically downward (\cref{fig:mse-training-results}b).
This behaviour is \emph{correct} with respect to the objective: once spikes are removed, point-wise errors shrink faster than they would by aligning spikes correctly, so the non-spiking region is a genuine local minimum of \gls{MSE}, not an optimisation artefact.
This confirms the intuition that pixel-wise distances on voltage traces are ill-suited to spiking data.

\section{Recovery-Radius Benchmark}\label{sec:results:baseline}

The per-loss subsections of \cref{sec:results:training} establish what gradient-based fitting can do at single, hand-chosen operating points.
The benchmark of \cref{sec:method:recovery-benchmark} aggregates this picture over $5\,\text{methods}\times 3\,\text{scenarios}\times 6\,\text{perturbations}\times 30\,\text{directions}=2700$ paired runs, producing a recovery-success curve per method and a single recovery radius per (scenario, method) pair.

\subsection{Headline result: success vs.\ perturbation}
\Cref{fig:recovery-radius} shows $P(\Gamma > 0.5)$ as a function of the perturbation scale $\delta$, with one panel per scenario and one curve per method.
A no-optimisation floor (\texttt{init\_floor}, the success rate of the perturbed initialisation evaluated without any update) is included as a reference; methods below this floor have regressed.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/recovery_radius_curves.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Recovery success $P(\Gamma > 0.5)$ as a function of the relative perturbation scale $\delta$, per scenario.
Each curve aggregates 30 paired random-direction perturbations; methods are evaluated on identical $(\theta^*+\delta u_k)$ initialisations within each scenario.
The $\Gamma > 0.5$ threshold follows \cref{sec:method:evaluation-metrics}; the dashed horizontal at $0.5$ marks the success-probability level used to define the recovery radius.
\texttt{init\_floor} reports $\Gamma$ at the perturbed initialisation with no optimisation applied.}\label{fig:recovery-radius}
\end{figure*}

Four observations stand out.
First, both Nelder--Mead variants dominate every gradient method on every scenario at every $\delta$: they have the largest recovery radii and the highest success rates inside them.
Second, gradient methods cluster tightly together at small $\delta$ (where any sensible loss is locally informative) and separate at large $\delta$ (where the loss landscape's non-spiking plateau --- characterised in \cref{sec:results:landscape} --- dominates the picture);
Van Rossum tracks Guarino on \texttt{tonic} and \texttt{initial\_bursting} but pulls ahead on \texttt{adaptation}, where temporal credit assignment via the exponential kernel matters more.
Third, \texttt{grad\_mse} sits at or below the \texttt{init\_floor} on every scenario for every $\delta$, confirming \cref{sec:results:mse}: pointwise voltage \gls{MSE} actively pulls the optimiser into the non-spiking subthreshold regime, so for most random directions training \emph{worsens} the fit relative to the perturbed initialisation.
Fourth, the two Nelder--Mead variants are indistinguishable: \texttt{nm\_guarino\_hard} and \texttt{nm\_guarino} report identical $\delta_{50}\in\{0.2,0.2,0.4\}$ and identical median $\Gamma_{0.05}=+1.00$ on every scenario, and their per-direction success counts match cell-for-cell to within one direction.
This is the expected outcome for a faithful soft approximation, and we read it as evidence that the soft features Guarino et al.~\cite{Guarino2025} introduced track the exact-feature Guarino loss closely enough that a derivative-free optimiser does not distinguish them.

\subsection{Soft vs.\ hard features for Nelder--Mead}\label{sec:results:nm-soft-vs-hard}
The Guarino objective is differentiable only because every feature it extracts has been replaced by a soft approximation (\cref{sec:feature-loss}).
The gradient methods require these approximations;
Nelder--Mead does not.
The \texttt{nm\_guarino} method retains the soft features so that the loss surface evaluated by Nelder--Mead is identical to the loss surface seen by \texttt{grad\_guarino}, isolating the optimiser effect from the objective.
The complementary configuration \texttt{nm\_guarino\_hard} drops the soft approximations entirely and runs the same Nelder--Mead simplex on the exact-feature Guarino loss --- the configuration Guarino et al.~\cite{Guarino2025} themselves use with evolutionary search.

The two variants produce identical recovery radii on every scenario and identical median $\Gamma$ at $\delta=0.05$ (\cref{tab:results-summary});
beyond the aggregate, their per-direction outcomes also match almost exactly, with the worst per-(scenario,\,$\delta$) discrepancy being a single direction out of 30.
Empirically, then, the soft features are a faithful surrogate for the true Guarino objective in the regime of perturbations this benchmark spans:
at the precision required to distinguish recovery success ($\Gamma>0.5$) and at the $50$-evaluation budget the simplex runs on, the soft objective and the hard objective induce the same simplex trajectory and the same recovered parameter set.

This rules out two natural objections to the headline comparison between gradient methods and \texttt{nm\_guarino}.
First, it is not the case that \texttt{nm\_guarino} ``cheats'' by being evaluated on a smoothed loss that the gradient methods would not see ---
on the hard loss it would not actually see (because it does not consume gradients), the simplex achieves the same radius.
Second, the gap between gradient methods and the simplex is not a soft-feature-bias artefact:
the soft features themselves are not biasing the objective enough to matter for the simplex, so they cannot be the dominant reason gradient descent fails to match the simplex's radius.
What remains, and what the next chapter argues drives the gap, is the surrogate gradient itself and the non-spiking plateau (\cref{sec:results:landscape}) over which it points in a biased direction.

\subsection{Per-parameter recovery error}
The success rate $P(\Gamma>0.5)$ aggregates over the entire parameter vector and tells us nothing about \emph{which} parameters individual methods recover.
\Cref{fig:recovery-per-param} breaks the same runs down by parameter, reporting the median absolute recovery error
$|\hat\theta_i - \theta^*_i| / (\theta_{\max,i} - \theta_{\min,i})$
at $\delta = 0.05$.
The membrane parameters $\{C_m, g_L, E_L, V_T, \Delta_T, V_r\}$ are the only optimisation targets in this benchmark (\cref{sec:method:recovery-benchmark});
the adaptation parameters $\{a, b, \tau_w\}$ are held at ground truth, so their bars are zero by construction and exist only to make explicit which axes the benchmark deliberately does not interrogate.
This separation is the bridge into the limitation discussed in the next section: \emph{nothing in this benchmark constrains adaptation parameters, because we already know from \cref{sec:results:training} that single-trace fits cannot identify them.}

\subsection{What the benchmark does not measure}
Three caveats determine how far the conclusions in \cref{sec:results:failure-modes,ch:discussion} are entitled to go.
\textbf{Synthetic targets.} The benchmark recovers parameters of a model from data generated by the same model, so it measures the optimisation problem in isolation, not the model--data mismatch one would face on real recordings; this is by design (\cref{sec:method:recovery-benchmark}) but means the radii reported here are upper bounds on what the same setup achieves experimentally.
\textbf{Single trace.} A single 100\,ms step current is not enough to pin down the adaptation parameters; the multi-trace extension of \cref{sec:training-stabilization} addresses this and is reported as a separate ablation rather than mixed into the benchmark.
\textbf{Fixed hyperparameters.} The configurations in \cref{tab:training-params} are the validated single-stage settings; per-scenario or per-$\delta$ HP tuning would shift radii up but is exactly the kind of tuning the user does not have when fitting a real cell. The radii reported here are therefore meant to characterise the methods as one would actually deploy them, not their best possible behaviour under unconstrained tuning.
