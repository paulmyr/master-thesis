\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports four classes of empirical results in service of the central question of the thesis: whether gradient-based parameter estimation can produce useful parameter updates for the \gls{AdEx} model.
\Cref{sec:results:verification} establishes that the forward model is correct, by comparing our \texttt{Jaxley} \gls{AdEx} channel and its surrogate-gradient variant against Brian2 on three reference parameter sets;
this is a precondition for the rest of the chapter, since it rules out the possibility that any later failure is an artefact of the dynamics rather than a property of the optimisation.
\Cref{sec:results:landscape} characterises the loss surfaces the optimiser actually descends, on a $20\times 20$ two-parameter sweep over all $\binom{8}{2}=28$ trainable pairs for each of four differentiable losses, supplemented by focused panels that isolate one diagnostic feature per loss.
The picture is a thin spiking valley flanked by a large non-spiking plateau on which the surrogate gradient supplies a direction not aligned with the valley --- a geometric prediction of the failure modes we later confirm in benchmarks.
\Cref{sec:results:training} reports what happens when an optimiser is run on those surfaces:
a worked example in which gradient-based recovery succeeds, followed by a recovery-radius benchmark over $2{,}700$ paired runs that compares three differentiable losses against two Nelder--Mead baselines on identical perturbed initialisations.
\Cref{sec:results:outlook} sketches what the same setup produces on a real recording as a proof of concept that motivates the experimental-trace direction in the future-work chapter.

\section{An AdEx Channel for Jaxley}\label{sec:results:verification}

The first concrete outcome of this thesis is a working \gls{AdEx} channel for \texttt{Jaxley}, validated against an established reference simulator.
Following the protocol of \cref{sec:method:adex:verification}, we compare our implementation to Brian2 \cite{Stimberg2019} on all three reference parameter sets from Naud et al.~\cite{Naud2008} --- tonic, adaptation and original --- which together cover the qualitatively distinct firing regimes \gls{AdEx} is meant to reproduce.

Across all three configurations, the two simulators produce identical spike counts, and the maximum pair-wise spike-timing difference stays below $1$\,ms.
\Cref{fig:brian_comparison} shows the side-by-side comparison of voltage traces, adaptation current $w$, and spike times.
The remaining sub-millisecond differences are what one would expect given that the two simulators use different integration schemes for the membrane equation, and they are well below the temporal resolution at which we later evaluate spike-timing accuracy via the coincidence factor $\Gamma$.

    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/adex_comparison_combined.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Comparison of our \texttt{Jaxley} \gls{AdEx} implementation against Brian2 across three reference parameter sets.
Left to right: tonic, adaptation and original parameters.
Tonic and adaptation parameters refer to the parameters provided by \cite{Naud2008}, original parameters are the parameters used in the original AdEx paper \cite{Brette2005}.

This validation matters for two reasons.
First, it establishes that an \gls{AdEx}-style simplified neuron --- with its discrete reset and exponential spike initiation --- can be expressed cleanly inside a framework otherwise designed for \gls{HH}-type compartmental models.
The same implementation pattern carries over to other simplified models such as \gls{LIF} and Izhikevich, so the contribution is not strictly limited to \gls{AdEx}.
Second, and more importantly for the rest of this chapter: every optimisation failure we report from here on can be discussed as a property of gradient-based training and loss-function design, rather than as a possible artefact of the underlying dynamics.
The forward model is not the bottleneck.

\section{Loss Function Analysis}\label{sec:results:landscape}

Before we report parameter-recovery numbers in \cref{sec:results:training}, this section characterises the geometry of the loss surfaces the optimiser actually descends.
The motivation is diagnostic: a flat success/failure number does not say \emph{why} a method fails, and the recovery-radius benchmark in \cref{sec:results:baseline} will show that all three differentiable losses fail in similar aggregate but for visibly different reasons.
We therefore look at the shape of each loss directly --- the position of the spiking valley, the extent of the non-spiking plateau, and the direction of the gradient on that plateau --- and use these geometric features to predict the failure modes the benchmark later confirms.

For every $\binom{8}{2}=28$ pair of trainable parameters we evaluate four differentiable losses (\gls{MSE}, \gls{SDTW}, the Guarino feature-based loss, and the Van Rossum distance) on a $20\times 20$ grid spanning the parameter bounds; the remaining six parameters are held at the Naud \texttt{adaptation} reference values of \cref{sec:method:adex:verification}.
The same grid evaluation produces a paired gradient field $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}$ via JAX autodiff, shown overlaid on the loss surface as a quiver.
The full $4\times 28$ triangle of loss and gradient panels is reproduced in \cref{sec:app:loss};
the subsections below pick the two pairs per loss that are most diagnostic of that loss's behaviour.

\subsection{Shared structure across losses}\label{sec:results:landscape:shared}

Three features recur across every loss surface we plot (\cref{sec:app:loss}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Narrow low-loss valleys.} The spiking region forms thin, curved valleys rather than isotropic basins.
        Valleys are often parallel to one axis (e.g.\ $g_L$--$E_L$ trades off along a single direction),
        meaning small updates along the wrong axis leave the loss unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Large non-spiking plateaus.} Substantial regions of parameter space yield a constant (or near-constant) loss because no spikes are produced and the loss reduces to a target-only constant.
        Soft spike-detection terms supply a residual gradient on these plateaus, but the gradient is small and its direction is not constrained by the true distance to the spiking valley.
  \item \textbf{Plateau gradient magnitude, not direction.} On the spike-aware losses (Guarino, Van Rossum) the surrogate supplies a small but non-zero gradient on the non-spiking plateau via $\sigma'(V - V_{\mathrm{th}})$.
        When read in bound-normalised coordinates the resulting plateau gradient is typically \emph{directionally informative} --- it points toward the spiking transition rather than along an iso-contour (\cref{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum}) --- but its magnitude is several orders below the gradient inside the spiking region, so without an adaptive step size an optimiser initialised on the plateau makes only negligible progress per epoch.
        \gls{MSE} is the structural exception (\cref{sec:results:landscape:mse}): the plateau gradient points actively \emph{away} from the spiking valley, because eliminating the simulated spikes lowers the pointwise voltage error more than aligning them ever can.
\end{itemize}

The three subsections that follow show what these shared features look like in each loss individually, and where the losses differ in ways that matter for gradient descent.

\subsection{Mean Squared Error: the non-spiking attractor}\label{sec:results:landscape:mse}

The \gls{MSE} surface is the most extreme case of the plateau pathology.
Across all 28 parameter pairs (\cref{sec:app:loss}) the surface separates into two distinct regimes:
a thin spiking valley aligned with the $g_L$--$E_L$ direction, and a much larger non-spiking basin whose loss value sits an order of magnitude \emph{below} the values inside the spiking valley.
The asymmetry is structural: when the simulated cell stops spiking, the squared difference between $V_{\mathrm{sim}}(t)$ and the target trace is dominated by the residual subthreshold mismatch only;
when it spikes but with imperfect timing, every misaligned spike contributes an error on the order of $(V_{\mathrm{peak}}-V_{\mathrm{rest}})^{2}$.
The non-spiking region is therefore a genuine minimum of the \gls{MSE} objective, not an artefact of the surrogate or of the soft approximations used elsewhere.

This geometry has an immediate empirical signature in training: starting from a perturbed but spiking initialisation, the \gls{MSE} optimiser deletes the simulated spikes rather than aligning them, because deleting spikes is the lower-loss move.
\Cref{fig:mse-training-results} shows the resulting trajectory on the Naud \texttt{adaptation} target: the recovered trace tracks the resting potential with no spikes, and the loss decreases monotonically across all $50$ epochs.

The bias of the plateau gradient in \cref{sec:results:landscape:shared} is therefore particularly damaging here:
\gls{MSE}'s gradient on the plateau does not merely fail to point toward the valley, it points actively away from it.
This geometry predicts the benchmark outcome reported in \cref{sec:results:baseline}, where \texttt{grad\_mse} sits at or below the no-optimisation floor on every scenario.
The behaviour is correct with respect to the objective:
the loss landscape itself rewards subthreshold solutions, and a working optimiser will find them.

\subsection{Guarino: a narrow valley with biased plateau gradients}\label{sec:results:landscape:guarino}

The Guarino feature-based loss replaces \gls{MSE}'s point-wise voltage comparison with a weighted sum of relative errors on eight discrete spike features (\cref{sec:method:opt:loss:feature-loss}), supplemented by a soft validity penalty whenever the simulated trace does not produce enough spikes to evaluate a feature.
Both changes target the failure mode diagnosed for \gls{MSE}: removing spikes is no longer cheaper than aligning them, because the missing-feature penalty contributes a large positive term to the loss whenever a feature cannot be evaluated.
The geometry near a spiking ground truth reflects this redesign.

\Cref{fig:guarino-landscape-focus} shows the loss and the gradient field on the $E_L$--$g_L$ plane around the Naud \texttt{adaptation} reference point, on a logarithmic scale that spans six orders of magnitude.
A narrow curved valley (dark purple, $\mathcal{L} \lesssim 10^{0}$) contains the ground truth and traces the locus where the leak equilibrium and conductance jointly produce the correct spike count and timing.
The valley is bounded on the upper-left by a sharp yellow ridge ($\mathcal{L}\sim 10^{5}$) along which the missing-feature penalty turns on as the simulated trace loses spikes;
beyond the ridge the loss settles onto a teal plateau ($\mathcal{L}\sim 10^{1}$) on which the simulated cell produces no spikes at all and the loss is dominated by the validity-penalty floor.
The two-regime structure --- a thin spiking valley flanked by a non-spiking plateau separated by a high-loss penalty ridge --- is the qualitative pattern we labelled in \cref{sec:results:landscape:shared}, and it recurs on every Guarino panel of the appendix grid (\cref{sec:app:loss}).

The right panel of \cref{fig:guarino-landscape-focus} makes the directional behaviour of the gradient explicit.
Three observations follow.
First, $\|\nabla\mathcal{L}\odot\Delta\theta\|_{2}$ spans five orders of magnitude across this single slice ($\sim 10^{2}$ in the plateau interior to $\sim 10^{7}$ on the penalty ridge);
a fixed learning rate cannot be appropriate in both regimes, which is the empirical motivation for Polyak step-size normalisation (\cref{sec:method:opt:stabilization:polyak}).
Second, on the non-spiking plateau the gradient field is approximately uniform --- arrows point in essentially the same south-east direction across a region tens of millivolts wide, and that direction is the descent direction toward the valley, not an iso-contour.
The plateau gradient is therefore informative directionally;
the magnitude on the plateau is $\sim 10^{4}$ below the ridge but still well above floating-point noise, so an optimiser initialised on the plateau receives a usable but slow signal back toward the valley.
Third, the largest gradients sit on the penalty ridge itself, not at the ground truth:
an optimiser whose trajectory crosses the ridge receives a sudden gradient signal whose direction is set by the surrogate kernel of the soft validity flag rather than by the spike-train error inside the valley.
This is the displacement of the surrogate-smoothed minimum from the true minimum that drives the surrogate-bias argument of \cref{sec:discussion:bias}, made geometrically visible.

The benchmark results in \cref{sec:results:baseline} are consistent with this picture.
\texttt{grad\_guarino} achieves $\delta_{50}=0.02$ on tonic and initial-bursting and fails the floor on adaptation;
that is, gradient-based recovery succeeds only when the perturbed initial point already sits close to or inside the valley.
The plateau gradient does carry a usable signal toward the valley, but at the magnitudes shown above the per-step parameter update is too small to traverse the plateau within the $25$-epoch budget the benchmark allows, and the optimiser stalls outside the valley.

\subsection{Van Rossum: a smoother loss path, a noisier gradient}\label{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum}

The Van Rossum distance (\cref{sec:method:opt:loss:van-rossum}) is the only loss in our comparison that supplies a smooth path from the spike train $s(t)$ to the loss value: the exponential kernel $K$ and the $L^{2}$ norm are themselves differentiable, so no softness hyperparameter is required to make the loss path smooth.
The Guarino loss compounds the surrogate gradient with a second smoothing layer (sigmoid-gated validity flags on top of soft spike-feature extraction);
Van Rossum strips that second layer away.
The geometric prediction is that the Van Rossum surface should be smoother in the spiking regime than Guarino's --- the kernel convolution averages over a $\tau_{\mathrm{kernel}}$-wide window, so small spike-timing perturbations no longer produce discrete penalty-ridge transitions.
The empirical picture in the bound-normalised gradient field is more nuanced.

\Cref{fig:vanrossum-grad-focus} shows the Van Rossum gradient field on the same $E_L$--$g_L$ slice and surrogate configuration as the Guarino panel of \cref{fig:guarino-landscape-focus} (SuperSpike, $\beta=10$), so the two figures differ only in the loss.

  \end{center}
  \caption{Van Rossum bound-normalised gradient-norm landscape with quiver overlay on the $E_L$--$g_L$ plane around the Naud \texttt{adaptation} reference point.
SuperSpike surrogate, $\beta=10$, matched to \cref{fig:guarino-landscape-focus}.

\end{figure}

Two contrasts with the Guarino panel stand out.
First, the missing-feature penalty ridge of \cref{fig:guarino-landscape-focus} is absent:
Van Rossum has no discrete penalty for losing a spike, so the loss climbs smoothly from valley to plateau across the diagonal transition rather than jumping by four orders of magnitude.
This is the kernel-smoothing effect the design argument predicts (\cref{sec:method:opt:loss:van-rossum}) and is consistent with the modestly larger basin of attraction observed for \texttt{grad\_vanrossum} on \texttt{adaptation} (\cref{sec:results:baseline}: $\delta_{50}=0.05$, the only gradient method to strictly exceed the no-optimisation floor on any scenario).
Second, the bound-normalised plateau gradient is much smaller for Van Rossum ($\sim 10^{-1}$) than for Guarino ($\sim 10^{2}$):
without the validity-flag penalty's soft-gated contribution, the only signal on the plateau comes from $\sigma'(V - V_{\mathrm{th}})$ in the brief window when the leak dynamics carry $V$ near threshold, which on the non-spiking plateau happens rarely or not at all.
The kernel-smoothing benefit on the spiking branch is therefore paid for by a weaker plateau gradient on the non-spiking branch ---
the optimiser inherits the same magnitude-bottleneck failure mode as Guarino, just from a different source.

The spiking region (lower-right of the diagonal) is \emph{not} the smooth basin the kernel-convolution argument would suggest in isolation.
Instead it is dominated by a high-contrast checkerboard of bright and dark patches whose magnitudes differ by an order of magnitude between neighbouring grid cells.
Read mechanistically:
the binary indicator $s(t)$ for any given spike depends on whether $V(t)$ enters the surrogate's $\sim 4/\beta$ mV active window, so a small parameter perturbation can shift one spike's threshold-crossing across a single timestep and add or remove an event from the trace.
The Van Rossum loss reads this as a discrete jump in $K \ast s(t)$, and the autodiff backward pass attributes the jump via \texttt{custom\_vjp} to the surrogate kernel at that timestep, producing a gradient at the affected grid cell that is large but uncorrelated with its neighbours' gradients.
The kernel convolution smooths the loss over timing perturbations of \emph{existing} spikes;
it does not smooth over spike-count changes, which is what dominates the region near and beyond the ground truth at any finite $\beta$.

The geometry above is computed under SuperSpike;
the surrogate family itself is a load-bearing choice, and not the choice the methodology of \cref{sec:method:surrogate-gradients:implementation} names as default.
\Cref{fig:vanrossum-surrogate-sweep} reports the same Van Rossum gradient field on the same slice for SuperSpike and sigmoid at three surrogate slopes $\beta\in\{5,10,25\}$.

The contrast is not subtle.
SuperSpike's heavy-tailed kernel keeps gradients alive across the entire slice at every $\beta$ tested:
the spiking-region magnitudes shrink from $\sim 10^{6}$ at $\beta=5$ to $\sim 10^{2}$ at $\beta=25$, and the plateau magnitude shrinks correspondingly, but the directional structure and the spatial coverage are preserved.
The sigmoid kernel, by contrast, exhibits the dead-neuron problem characterised by Gygax \& Zenke~\cite{Gygax2025} in pure geometric form:
even at the broad setting $\beta=5$ the upper-left non-spiking plateau is black (gradient at or below numerical noise);
at $\beta=10$ the dead zone has expanded to cover most of the slice;
at $\beta=25$ only a sparse pattern of isolated grid cells retains any gradient at all, with the remaining ``alive'' cells reporting magnitudes near $10^{-15}$ --- two to three orders of magnitude below the float32 epsilon.
This is the empirical reason the recovery-radius benchmark uses SuperSpike $\beta=6$ for the gradient methods (\cref{tab:training-params}) rather than the sigmoid $\beta=5$ named as the verification default in \cref{sec:method:surrogate-gradients:implementation}:
at the benchmark's perturbation scales, the sigmoid would land most random initialisations on dead-zone grid cells where no descent step is possible regardless of the loss function used.

\subsubsection*{Surrogate-bypass via voltage features: an asymmetry between Guarino and Van Rossum}

The dead-zone diagnosis above admits an exception that is empirically informative.
Compare \cref{fig:vanrossum-surrogate-sweep}'s sigmoid $\beta=10$ Van Rossum panel against the same surrogate setting under the Guarino loss (\cref{fig:guarino-sigmoid-vs-vrsubthresh}, left): under Van Rossum the slice is dead almost everywhere;
under Guarino the slice is alive ($\sim 10^{0}$--$10^{2}$ in bound-normalised units) with a uniform downward-pointing plateau gradient that is the geometric mirror of its SuperSpike counterpart.
The two losses share the same forward simulation, the same surrogate kernel, and the same $V(t)$ trajectory.
What separates them is the loss decomposition.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/loss_landscape_focus_guarino_E_L_g_L_grad2d_sigmoid_10.pdf}\hfill
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/loss_landscape_focus_vanrossum_E_L_g_L_grad2d_sigmoid_10_weightsubthreshold_03.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Surrogate-bypass via direct voltage features.
Both panels use the sigmoid surrogate at $\beta=10$ --- the setting that produces a near-total dead zone for plain Van Rossum (cf.\ \cref{fig:vanrossum-surrogate-sweep}, bottom-middle).
Left: Guarino loss.
Right: Van Rossum with $\lambda_{\mathrm{sub}}=0.3$ (\cref{eq:vr-sub}).
In both, the entire slice carries non-zero gradient with magnitudes in the $10^{-1}$--$10^{1}$ range (Van Rossum) and $10^{-2}$--$10^{2}$ range (Guarino), and the plateau quiver points uniformly toward the spiking transition.
The diagonal stripes visible in the right panel are iso-contours of the subthreshold equilibrium voltage $E_L + I/g_L$ --- direct evidence that the rescuing signal is the voltage path, not the spike path.}\label{fig:guarino-sigmoid-vs-vrsubthresh}
\end{figure}

Of Guarino's eight features, seven are extracted from the binary spike train $s(t)$ and therefore route their parameter gradient through the surrogate $\partial s / \partial V$ (the same path that VR uses).
The eighth is the voltage at the end of the stimulus window, $V_{\mathrm{stimend}} = V[T_{\mathrm{stim}}]$, which is read directly off the simulated voltage trace and routes its gradient through the leak dynamics with no surrogate in the path:
on the non-spiking plateau the reset never fires ($s\equiv 0$ implies $V_{\mathrm{new}} = (1-s)V = V$ at every step), so $\partial V[T_{\mathrm{stim}}]/\partial \theta$ is the well-defined gradient of an exponential relaxation toward $E_L + I/g_L$.
This term is small in absolute loss compared to the missing-feature penalty in the spiking-failure regime, but it is the \emph{only} term whose gradient survives the sigmoid's exponential suppression on the plateau, so it is what \cref{fig:guarino-sigmoid-vs-vrsubthresh} (left) is showing.

Van Rossum has no comparable term in its default form (\cref{eq:vr-discrete}): the entire loss is a function of $K\ast s$, so when $s$ is dead the loss is dead.
The optional subthreshold term of \cref{eq:vr-sub}, included as a small-positive option in the methodology specifically ``so the optimiser is not stuck [on the no-spike plateau]'' (\cref{sec:method:opt:loss:van-rossum}), is the surrogate-bypass that Guarino enjoys by construction.
\Cref{fig:guarino-sigmoid-vs-vrsubthresh} (right) shows what happens when that term is turned on at $\lambda_{\mathrm{sub}}=0.3$ under the same dead-surrogate setting: the slice is alive everywhere, and the diagonal stripes in the gradient magnitude trace iso-contours of $V_{\mathrm{rest}} = E_L + I/g_L$ --- exactly the dependence one would expect from a voltage-MAE term.

The reading that follows is that ``the surrogate is dead'' and ``the gradient is dead'' are not the same statement.
Whenever a loss has any term that depends on $V(t)$ without passing through $s(t)$, that term provides a surrogate-free signal path which dominates the gradient on the non-spiking plateau where surrogate-routed terms vanish.
Guarino's $V_{\mathrm{stimend}}$ feature provides this path automatically;
Van Rossum's $\lambda_{\mathrm{sub}}$ option provides it on demand;
\gls{MSE} consists \emph{entirely} of this kind of term, which is exactly why \gls{MSE} is the only loss in our comparison that has a non-zero gradient on the plateau \emph{but pointing the wrong way} (\cref{sec:results:landscape:mse}).
The shared structure across the three losses is therefore richer than ``surrogate-mediated spike-train comparison plus a soft tweak per loss'';
it is a layered design in which a slow, voltage-based fallback term keeps the optimiser alive in the dead-neuron regime, and a fast, surrogate-based spike-aware term takes over once the cell starts firing.
The discussion in \cref{sec:futureWork} returns to this observation in arguing for hybrid objectives.

\subsubsection*{Why we cannot fit $\tau_w$: gradient asymmetry on the $(a, \tau_w)$ plane}

The recovery-radius benchmark holds the adaptation parameters $(a, b, \tau_w)$ at ground truth and trains only the six membrane parameters (\cref{sec:method:opt:recovery-benchmark:scope}).
The reason is empirical: in the per-loss training notebooks of \cref{sec:results:training} we were unable to recover $\tau_w$ from a single 100\,ms step-current trace under any of the differentiable losses, even when $a$ and $b$ were trainable in the same run.
\Cref{fig:vanrossum-a-tauw} shows the bound-normalised gradient field on the $(a, \tau_w)$ plane around the Naud \texttt{adaptation} reference point ($a=2$\,nS, $\tau_w=300$\,ms), under Van Rossum with SuperSpike $\beta=10$.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/loss_landscape_focus_vanrossum_a_tau_w_grad2d_superspike_10.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Van Rossum bound-normalised gradient field on the $a$--$\tau_w$ plane in the \texttt{adaptation} scenario (ground truth $a=2$\,nS, $\tau_w=300$\,ms).
SuperSpike surrogate, $\beta=10$.
The colour scale spans six orders of magnitude in bound-normalised sensitivity but the quiver is dominated by the horizontal component throughout: $|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial a \cdot \Delta a| \gg |\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial \tau_w \cdot \Delta \tau_w|$ across the whole slice.
The optimiser reads any path in this plane primarily as motion in $a$;
$\tau_w$ acquires gradient only via the granular spike-count noise pattern that is uncorrelated with the actual ground-truth location.}\label{fig:vanrossum-a-tauw}
\end{figure}

The figure makes a finding visible that the recovery-radius benchmark deliberately holds out of scope.
The granular spike-count texture of \cref{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum} reappears here, but with one striking change: the quiver is dominated by the horizontal ($a$) component at essentially every grid cell, even after bound-normalisation puts the two axes on the same dimensionless scale.
This is a real geometric property, not the unit-scaling artefact of the raw quiver discussed in \cref{sec:results:landscape:shared};
it says that within a single short step-current trace, the loss responds to changes in the subthreshold adaptation strength $a$ much more strongly than to changes in the adaptation timescale $\tau_w$.
Mechanically, $a$ enters $dw/dt$ via $a(V - E_L)$ at every timestep, so its effect on the spike train accumulates linearly in stimulus duration;
$\tau_w$ enters via the exponential decay of $w$ between spikes, so its effect is detectable only over time windows comparable to $\tau_w$ itself, which at the Naud value of $300$\,ms is of the same order as the entire $100$\,ms simulation window.
A single trace simply does not last long enough for $\tau_w$ to leave a strong signature on the loss, regardless of how cleverly the loss is constructed.

The implication threads through the rest of the chapter.
\Cref{sec:results:baseline} restricts the benchmark to membrane parameters precisely because the gradient signal on $\tau_w$ is too weak to support meaningful recovery from this stimulus protocol;
the discussion in \cref{sec:discussion:nm} attributes the gradient methods' failure on $\tau_w$ to ``weak identifiability from a single trace'' rather than to any specific failure of the surrogate or the optimiser;
and the future-work direction in \cref{sec:futureWork} that calls for multi-trace constraints (\cref{sec:method:opt:stabilization:multi-trace}) is precisely the protocol-level intervention this asymmetry argues for.
A longer trace, or several traces with different stimulus amplitudes that drive $w$ over different ranges, would make the $\tau_w$ axis informative;
a different loss function, applied to the same single 100\,ms trace, would not.

\section{Parameter Recovery}\label{sec:results:training}

We now turn from the geometry of the loss surfaces (\cref{sec:results:landscape}) to what happens when an optimiser actually descends them.
Throughout this section the ground truth is one of the Naud reference parameter sets (\texttt{tonic}, \texttt{adaptation}, or \texttt{initial\_bursting})~\cite{Naud2008},
the target voltage trace is generated with the validated \texttt{Jaxley} \gls{AdEx} implementation of \cref{sec:results:verification},
and the trainable parameters are perturbed before optimisation.
Because the optimiser has access to the same forward model that produced the target, any failure to recover is attributable to the loss function and the optimisation procedure rather than to model--data mismatch.
The trainable parameters are restricted to the six membrane parameters $\{C_m, g_L, E_L, V_T, \Delta_T, V_r\}$;
the adaptation parameters $\{a, b, \tau_w\}$ are held at their ground-truth values throughout, for the reasons established in \cref{sec:method:opt:recovery-benchmark:scope} and made geometrically explicit in \cref{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum}.

The section is organised in two parts.
\Cref{sec:results:positive-example} presents a single worked example in which gradient-based recovery succeeds cleanly --- enough to establish that the approach is viable in principle, but already revealing the per-parameter compensation that the systematic results will quantify.
\Cref{sec:results:baseline} then reports the recovery-radius benchmark: $2{,}700$ paired runs across three firing regimes, six perturbation scales and thirty random directions, comparing the three differentiable losses against two Nelder--Mead baselines on matched initialisations.
The benchmark is the headline result of the chapter, and the picture it produces is uncomfortable:
on every scenario and every perturbation scale, gradient-based recovery falls at or below the no-optimisation floor on two losses, and exceeds it by one $\delta$-tier on a single (loss, scenario) cell;
Nelder--Mead on the same objective recovers reliably out to two to four $\delta$-tiers further.
The geometric features of \cref{sec:results:landscape} predict this pattern in advance.

\subsection{A Worked Example: Recovering a Tonic AdEx Cell}\label{sec:results:positive-example}

Before turning to the per-loss breakdown and the systematic benchmark, we present one configuration in which gradient-based fitting succeeds cleanly.
The ground truth is the Naud tonic parameter set (\cref{sec:method:adex:verification}); the six trainable membrane parameters $\{C_m, g_L, V_r, V_T, E_L, \Delta_T\}$ are perturbed by approximately $15\,\%$ of their bound range, and the optimiser is run for $50$ epochs of single-stage training under the Guarino feature-based loss (\cref{sec:method:opt:loss:feature-loss}).
The adaptation parameters $\{a, b, \tau_w\}$ are held at ground truth, matching the scope fixed for the recovery-radius benchmark in \cref{sec:method:opt:recovery-benchmark:scope}.
\Cref{fig:guarino-synthetic-recovery} reports the outcome.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/guarino_synthetic_recovery}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Single-stage recovery of a tonic \gls{AdEx} cell under the Guarino loss.
Top: voltage traces of the ground truth (black, $9$ spikes), the perturbed initialisation (orange, $7$ spikes) and the recovered fit (blue, $9$ spikes), with the corresponding spike raster below.
Bottom left: training loss across $50$ epochs; the dashed line marks the epoch at which the best parameters were retained.

At the trace level the recovery is visually convincing.
The perturbed initialisation produces $12$ spikes against the target's $9$ and misses most spike times by several milliseconds; after training the recovered trace matches the ground truth spike-for-spike and yields a coincidence factor of $\Gamma = 1.00$ at the $\Delta = 2\,\text{ms}$ tolerance fixed in \cref{sec:method:opt:evaluation-metrics}.
The loss curve descends monotonically over roughly one order of magnitude, with the steepest drop concentrated between epochs $35$ and $45$ where the optimiser successfully finds the right spike count.

The per-parameter panel tells a more nuanced story.
Two of the six trainable parameters --- $C_m$ and $V_{\text{reset}}$ --- tighten substantially towards their ground-truth values.
However, other parameters change marginally or drift further away from the ground truth.
Especially $g_L$'s final error greatly exceeds the initial perturbation.
The optimiser is therefore not recovering the parameter vector pointwise;
it is settling into a local minima combination that reproduces the target spike train while trading error across correlated axes.
The loss-landscape evidence behind this compensation is taken up in \cref{sec:results:landscape}, and its implications for parameter identifiability are discussed in \cref{ch:discussion}.

This success is not generic.
The same loss, the same hyperparameters and the same ground-truth parameter set fail to recover a comparable fit once the perturbation is increased, the firing regime is changed, or the adaptation parameters are made trainable.
The recovery-radius benchmark of \cref{sec:results:baseline} quantifies how quickly the success region closes;
the geometric counterpart in \cref{sec:results:landscape} explains why.

\subsection{Recovery-Radius Benchmark}\label{sec:results:baseline}
The worked example of \cref{sec:results:positive-example} shows that gradient-based recovery is possible in at least one configuration; it does not establish how robust the recovery is, nor how it compares against a derivative-free baseline on the same objective.
The recovery-radius benchmark of \cref{sec:method:opt:recovery-benchmark} answers both questions on a single instrument: it aggregates parameter recovery over $5\,\text{methods}\times 3\,\text{scenarios}\times 6\,\text{perturbations}\times 30\,\text{directions}=2700$ paired runs, producing a recovery-success curve per method and a single recovery radius per (scenario, method) pair.

\subsubsection{Headline result: success vs.\ perturbation}
\Cref{fig:recovery-radius} shows $P(\Gamma > 0.5)$ as a function of the perturbation scale $\delta$, with one panel per scenario and one curve per method.
A no-optimisation floor (\texttt{init\_floor}, the success rate of the perturbed initialisation evaluated without any update) is included as a reference; methods below this floor have regressed.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/recovery_radius_curves.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Recovery success $P(\Gamma > 0.5)$ as a function of the relative perturbation scale $\delta$, per scenario.
Each curve aggregates 30 paired random-direction perturbations; methods are evaluated on identical $(\theta^*+\delta u_k)$ initialisations within each scenario.
The $\Gamma > 0.5$ threshold follows \cref{sec:method:opt:evaluation-metrics}; the dashed horizontal at $0.5$ marks the success-probability level used to define the recovery radius.
\texttt{init\_floor} reports $\Gamma$ at the perturbed initialisation with no optimisation applied.}\label{fig:recovery-radius}
\end{figure*}

Four observations stand out.
First, all gradient methods perform similar or worse than the initialisation floor.
Even for tiny perturbations, the gradient approach struggled to faithfully recover original parameters.
Second, both Nelder--Mead variants dominate every gradient method on every scenario at every $\delta$: they have the largest recovery radii and the highest success rates inside them.
Third, \texttt{grad\_mse} performed consistently the worst.
This follows from the landscape evidence in \cref{sec:results:landscape:mse}: pointwise voltage \gls{MSE} actively pulls the optimiser into the non-spiking subthreshold regime, so for most random directions training \emph{worsens} the fit relative to the perturbed initialisation.
Fourth, the two Nelder--Mead variants are indistinguishable: \texttt{nm\_guarino} (soft features, matching the surface \texttt{grad\_guarino} sees) and \texttt{nm\_guarino\_hard} (exact features, the configuration Guarino et al.~\cite{Guarino2025} use with evolutionary search) report identical $\delta_{50}\in\{0.2,0.2,0.4\}$ and identical median $\Gamma_{0.05}=+1.00$ on every scenario, with per-direction outcomes differing by at most one direction out of 30.

\subsubsection{Soft vs.\ hard features for Nelder--Mead}\label{sec:results:nm-soft-vs-hard}
Since the soft and hard variants are empirically indistinguishable at this evaluation budget, the soft features are a faithful surrogate for the true Guarino objective in the perturbation regime this benchmark spans.
This rules out two objections to the gradient-vs-simplex comparison: \texttt{nm\_guarino} is not advantaged by a smoothed loss (the simplex reaches the same radius on the hard objective), and the gap between gradient methods and the simplex is not a soft-feature-bias artefact.
What remains, and what the next chapter argues drives the gap, is the surrogate gradient itself and the non-spiking plateau (\cref{sec:results:landscape}) over which it points in a biased direction.

\subsubsection{What the benchmark does not measure}
Three caveats determine how far the conclusions in \cref{ch:discussion} are entitled to go.
\textbf{Synthetic targets.} The benchmark recovers parameters of a model from data generated by the same model, so it measures the optimisation problem in isolation, not the model--data mismatch one would face on real recordings; this is by design (\cref{sec:method:opt:recovery-benchmark}) but means the radii reported here are upper bounds on what the same setup achieves experimentally.
\textbf{Single trace.} A single 100\,ms step current is not enough to pin down the adaptation parameters; the multi-trace extension of \cref{sec:method:opt:stabilization:multi-trace} is the natural address but lies outside the benchmark's scope.
\textbf{Fixed hyperparameters.} The configurations in \cref{tab:training-params} are the validated single-stage settings; per-scenario or per-$\delta$ HP tuning would shift radii up but is exactly the kind of tuning the user does not have when fitting a real cell. The radii reported here are therefore meant to characterise the methods as one would actually deploy them, not their best possible behaviour under unconstrained tuning.

\section{Outlook: Training on Voltage Recordings}\label{sec:results:outlook}

The recovery-radius benchmark of \cref{sec:results:baseline} fits synthetic targets generated by the same forward model used in optimisation;
it measures the optimisation problem in isolation but says nothing about how the setup behaves on a real recording.
This section reports a single proof-of-concept fit on an experimental trace from the Johansson \& Silberberg striatal recordings~\cite{Johansson2020}, intended to bound the rest of the chapter rather than to extend the benchmark to real data.
The full benchmark on experimental traces is left as future work (\cref{sec:futureWork}).

We fit the IDthresh recording at $I=421$\,pA (trace identifier 553) from the cell used as a worked example by the existing fitting pipeline in our codebase.
The target is the experimental voltage trace cropped to a $300$\,ms window around the stimulus, in which the cell fires $8$ spikes at $\sim 27$\,Hz.
The same six membrane parameters as the recovery-radius benchmark of \cref{sec:results:baseline} are trained;
the adaptation parameters $(a, b, \tau_w)$ are held at initial values, for the same identifiability reasons developed in \cref{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum}.
The loss is Guarino feature-based with the per-cell weights from the existing single-trace pipeline (heavy weight on the first three spike times and on the firing frequency, light weight on the spike-count regulariser);
the surrogate is SuperSpike $\beta=25$ rather than the broad $\beta=6$ used in the benchmark.
The sharp surrogate is usable here because, as established in \cref{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum}, the Guarino loss has a $V_{\mathrm{stimend}}$ term whose gradient bypasses the surrogate;
the dead-zone failure mode that would kill a pure spike-train loss at $\beta=25$ does not apply.
The optimiser is Adam at learning rate $10^{-3}$, sigmoid bound reparameterisation, $80$ epochs;
\texttt{return\_best=True} keeps the lowest-loss checkpoint.

Two ingredients make the result possible.
First, the initial parameter vector was hand-tuned by coarse manual search until the unoptimised forward simulation produced $7$ spikes against the target's $8$ at $\Gamma_{\mathrm{init}}=+0.411$ ($\Delta=4$\,ms tolerance) --- already above the success threshold the rest of the chapter uses.
Second, the gradient run is best understood as a refinement step on top of that hand-picked starting point rather than as a basin search;
the largest parameter movement during training is $|\Delta C_m| \approx 0.17$\,pF on a bound range of $230$\,pF (about one part in $1{,}300$), and the other five parameters move by even less.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/real_singletrace_553_fit}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Single-trace gradient-based fit of an IDthresh recording ($I=421$\,pA, 8 target spikes).
Top: voltage overlay before training --- the hand-tuned initial parameters produce $7$ spikes at $\Gamma_{\mathrm{init}}=+0.411$.
Middle: voltage overlay after $30$ Adam epochs --- $8$ spikes at $\Gamma=+1.000$ (all eight aligned within the $\Delta=4$\,ms coincidence window).
Bottom: spike raster for the three traces.
Training moved the six trainable membrane parameters by less than one part in a thousand of their bound width;

\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:real-singletrace-fit} reports the outcome.
The trained simulation reproduces the experimental spike train spike-for-spike, with $\Gamma=+1.000$ at $\Delta=4$\,ms tolerance (a more lenient window than the $\Delta=2$\,ms used for the synthetic benchmark, justified here by the increased timing jitter of experimental recordings) --- the maximum coincidence factor attainable.
The training loss falls from $3.28$ to $0.30$ over the first $30$ epochs and then becomes noisy as discrete spike-count transitions interact with the sharp surrogate's narrow active window;
\texttt{return\_best} pins the reported result to the epoch-$29$ minimum.
Subthreshold trajectories between spikes diverge visibly from the recording --- the cell's biophysical complexity is not captured by six membrane parameters --- but the spike count, firing frequency and spike timings all match within the precision the coincidence factor measures.
For context, Jolivet et al.~\cite{Jolivet2008} report $\Gamma \approx 0.82$ as state-of-the-art performance for fitting simplified neuron models to real recordings;
the value we report here is on a single trace, in a regime where the initial point was already very close, and should be read as a sanity check that gradient-based refinement is feasible on real data rather than as a competitive benchmark number.

The result intentionally does not refute the recovery-radius benchmark's negative conclusion.
The benchmark says gradient descent does not enlarge the basin of attraction relative to the Nelder--Mead simplex on the synthetic recovery problem;
this section says that, given a basin-of-attraction-sized initial guess (whether hand-tuned, NM-derived, or otherwise pre-conditioned), gradient descent can polish it to a high-precision fit on a real recording.
The two findings are consistent and together suggest the deployment workflow described in \cref{sec:futureWork}: derivative-free search for the basin, gradient descent for the refinement.
Three caveats bound the generality of the single fit reported here.

\textbf{Initialisation was hand-tuned, not searched.}
The hand-tuning landed us at $\Gamma_{\mathrm{init}}=+0.411$ before training even began.
Reaching that starting point required manual constraint reasoning ($E_L \approx V_{\mathrm{rest}}$, $g_L$ small enough that $V_\infty > V_T - \Delta_T$, $C_m/g_L \approx $ target ISI/$2$) plus a coarse grid search;
neither step was automated.
The conclusion's recommendation to seed gradient descent from a Nelder--Mead fit (\cref{sec:futureWork}) would replace this manual step with the simplex baseline that already wins the recovery-radius benchmark.

\textbf{Single trace at a single stimulus amplitude.}
The fit constrains the membrane parameters from one $300$\,ms step-current trace.
A single trace contains no information that can disambiguate parameter combinations producing the same firing pattern under one stimulus from those that diverge under another (\cref{sec:method:opt:stabilization:multi-trace}), and gives no leverage on the adaptation parameters (\cref{sec:results:landscape:vanrossum}).
A multi-trace extension following the $\texttt{vmap}$-batched simulation pattern of \texttt{training\_synthetic\_multitrace.ipynb} is the natural next step.

\textbf{Hyperparameters were tuned per-fit, not transferred from the benchmark.}
The surrogate ($\beta=25$ vs the benchmark's $\beta=6$), the optimiser (Adam vs Polyak), and the loss-weight vector were all chosen to fit this cell on this trace.
Cell-to-cell generalisation would require either a per-cell tuning protocol or a single robust configuration validated across a population of recordings;
neither is in scope for this thesis.

The conclusion (\cref{ch:conclusionsAndFutureWork}) returns to this proof of concept when arguing that the gradient-based pipeline's contribution lies in refinement rather than in basin search.
